The biggest chunk (40%) is conclusions. These are the 2–3 mark questions where you need a trend, a statistic (r, p, mean or SD) and psychological reasoning. Practise writing those out in full.

Statistics you must know

  • Central tendency: mean, median (and mode)
  • Dispersion in a sample: range, interquartile range, standard deviation
  • Inference about the population: standard error, confidence intervals
  • Correlation: Pearson r
  • Inferential tests: two-sample t-test (paired and unpaired), p-values

Five habits that win marks

  1. Show all working for "calculate". Working is usually worth a mark on its own.
  2. Quote the numbers. "Deep has the greater SD (20.5 vs 11.5)" beats "deep is more spread out".
  3. Include units (µm, ms, %, hours) and round as asked.
  4. Compare p to 0.05 in writing: "p = 0.01 < 0.05".
  5. Link to psychology in every 3-mark conclusion: name the brain structure or memory theory.
Stats toolkit

Each statistic: what it is, how to get it, how to write it

In the exam you calculate the simple ones (mean, median, range) and interpret the harder ones (SD, r, p), which are usually given to you in a table.

Measures of central tendency

Mean (x̄)

The average. Add every score and divide by how many there are.

x̄ = (x₁ + x₂ + … + xₙ) ÷ n

Example (L15): (15 + 22 + 27 + 31 + 36 + 39 + 47) ÷ 7 = 217 ÷ 7 = 31

[1] working  [1] answer

Trap: the mean is pulled toward outliers. One huge or tiny value changes it a lot.

Median

The middle score once the data is in order.

  • Odd n: the middle value, at position (n + 1) ÷ 2.
  • Even n: average the two middle values.

Example: 9, 3, 8, 2, 6, 2 → 2, 2, 3, 6, 8, 9 → (3 + 6) ÷ 2 = 4.5

Why use it: not affected by outliers, so it's better than the mean when data has extreme values.

Mode

The most frequent score. A dataset can have no mode or more than one.

Example (L15 Q6): participants 3, 7 and 10 all smoked 13 cigarettes, the highest value. When several people share a value, name all of them.

Reading the mean formula: what x₁ + x₂ + … means

x̄ = (x₁ + x₂ + … + xₙ) ÷ n
SymbolMeaning
x₁, x₂, x₃ …Your scores, one by one. The small number just counts which score it is: x₁ is the 1st, x₂ the 2nd, x₃ the 3rd.
…Keep going through all the scores.
xₙThe last score.
nHow many scores there are. Count them.
x̄"x-bar", the mean.

The numbers go up to however many scores there are. That last number is n.

  • Dataset 1 (motor neurons): 11 participants → x₁ to x₁₁, n = 11
  • Dataset 3 (TV): 6 participants → x₁ to x₆, n = 6
  • L15 stress example: 7 categories → x₁ to x₇, n = 7

Example with 4 scores (10, 12, 8, 14):

x̄ = (x₁ + x₂ + x₃ + x₄) ÷ 4 = (10 + 12 + 8 + 14) ÷ 4 = 44 ÷ 4 = 11

In the exam you don't write the x₁, x₂ labels. Write the real numbers: "(10 + 12 + 8 + 14) ÷ 4 = 44 ÷ 4 = 11". That is full working for both marks. Before dividing, check that the count of numbers you added matches n.

Measures of dispersion (spread, variability, uncertainty)

Dispersion describes how much the data values differ from each other and from the mean. More spread means more variability and more uncertainty, so the data is less consistent.

Range
Range = highest − lowest

"Sequence from least to greatest variability": calculate the range of each subgroup, then list them smallest → largest, showing each value.

Seen twice in your class material: sometimes every subgroup has the same range (L15 Q7: all = 2; practice Q15: all = 14). Then the answer is "they have equal variability". Show each calculation.

Trap: range uses only two numbers, so one outlier distorts it.

Interquartile range (IQR)
IQR = Q3 − Q1
  1. Put the data in order.
  2. Find the median; split the data into a lower and upper half (leave the median out if n is odd).
  3. Q1 = median of the lower half; Q3 = median of the upper half.

The spread of the middle 50%. Not affected by outliers.

Example: 2, 4, 5, 7, 9, 11, 12 → lower 2, 4, 5 (Q1 = 4); upper 9, 11, 12 (Q3 = 11); IQR = 7.

Standard deviation (SD)

The average distance of scores from the mean. Usually given; you interpret it.

s = √[ Σ(x − x̄)² ÷ (n − 1) ]
  • Larger SD = scores spread further from the mean = more variability, more uncertainty, less consistent.
  • Smaller SD = scores clustered around the mean = more consistent, more reliable.

Model answer: "Deep processing has the greatest dispersion (SD = 20.5) compared with shallow (SD = 11.5), a difference of 9."

Using a sample to make an inference about the population

Standard error (SE)
SE = SD ÷ √n

How far the sample mean is likely to be from the true population mean.

  • Smaller SE = more precise estimate of the population mean.
  • Bigger sample (n) → smaller SE. This is why small samples (like N = 6) are a limitation.

Example: SD = 12, n = 9 → SE = 12 ÷ 3 = 4

95% confidence interval (CI)
95% CI = x̄ ± 1.96 × SE  (≈ x̄ ± 2 × SE)

The range we are 95% confident contains the true population mean.

  • Narrow CI = less uncertainty. Wide CI = more uncertainty.
  • If two conditions' CIs don't overlap, the difference is likely to be real (significant). If they overlap a lot, it may be due to chance.

Example: x̄ = 50, SE = 4 → 50 ± 7.84 → 42.16 to 57.84

Relationships and significance

Pearson's correlation coefficient (r)

Measures the direction and strength of a linear relationship between two variables. Ranges from −1 to +1.

|r| valueStrength
0.7 – 1.0Strong
0.4 – 0.69Moderate
0.1 – 0.39Weak
below 0.1None / negligible
  • Sign = direction. + means both rise together; − means one rises as the other falls.
  • Size = strength. Ignore the sign when comparing: −0.89 is stronger than +0.72.
  • Correlation does not prove causation. That's why conclusions say "may affect".

Strength bands vary slightly between textbooks. Use your teacher's if different.

p-value (statistical significance)

The probability that the result happened by chance. Compare it to 0.05.

p < 0.05Statistically significant. Unlikely to be due to chance. Reject the null hypothesis.
p ≥ 0.05Not significant. Could be due to chance. Retain the null hypothesis.

Model answer (2 marks): "There is a statistically significant difference between deep and shallow processing, as p = 0.01 < 0.05 [1], meaning the difference in test performance is unlikely to be due to chance [1]."

The L15 slides wrote "0.003 < 0.005". The cut-off you compare to is 0.05 unless the question says otherwise.

t-tests: paired or unpaired?

A t-test checks whether the difference between two means is significant (it gives the p-value).

Paired t-testUnpaired t-test
Same participants do both conditions (repeated measures). Two sets of scores from the same people. Different participants in each condition (independent groups design).
e.g. the same students recall words in silence and with music; the same 6 students watch educative and non-educative TV. e.g. a Controlled Group and a separate Test Group of smokers.
Trends on graphs
  • Positive: as X increases, Y increases.
  • Negative: as X increases, Y decreases.
  • Linear: points follow roughly a straight line.
  • Rate: a steeper line increases or decreases faster.
  • Interpolate = read a value inside the data range. Extrapolate = extend the trend beyond it.
  • Outlier: a value far from the rest (e.g. 1300 µm in Dataset 1).

Prediction hint from class: draw a straight trendline from the first to the last data point and extend it, even if a line is already drawn.

Limitations and uncertainty (for "identify a limitation" questions)

  • Small sample size (e.g. N = 6) → results may not generalise to the population; larger SE, wider CI.
  • Outliers distort the mean and range.
  • Large SD → high variability, less consistent and less reliable results.
  • Correlation ≠ causation; other (confounding) variables may explain the relationship.
  • p ≥ 0.05 → difference may be due to chance.
  • Overlapping CIs → the conditions may not really differ.

The 40% section

How to write "draw a conclusion"

1 · Trend

State the relationship with numbers from the graph.

"As weekly hours studied increase, mean test scores increase (from ~25% at 1 hour to ~90% at 7 hours)."

2 · Data

Back it up with r (strength and direction) and/or p (significance), means or SD.

"This is supported by a strong positive correlation (r = +0.96)."

3 · Reasoning

Explain why using psychology: a brain structure or memory theory.

"Deep processing enhances semantic encoding, which strengthens memory traces, leading to higher recall (Craik & Lockhart)."

Read what the question is asking about. In L18 three "conclusion" questions used the same graph but wanted different things:

Question says…Look atInclude
"…how deep processing may affect test scores" (3 marks)One condition onlyTrend + r + psych reasoning
"…the statistical significance of differences between the two conditions" (2 marks)The p-valuep compared to 0.05 + "significant / unlikely due to chance"
"…the relationship between hours studied and score" (2 marks)Both lines together, the overall patternOverall direction + r
"…how the conditions differ"Means, SDs, pMean and SD of each + p-value
"…language practice as a whole"All conditionsWhich task is higher overall + overall trend + r
Sentence starters
  • "Figure 1 shows a positive/negative trend: as ___ increases, ___ increases/decreases (from ___ to ___)."
  • "This is supported by a strong positive/negative correlation (r = ___)."
  • "The result is statistically significant (p = ___ < 0.05), so it is unlikely to be due to chance."
  • "This may occur because the [brain structure / memory process] is responsible for ___, so ___."

Psychological reasoning bank

Brain & memory facts for your third mark

Every 3-mark conclusion needs a "this may occur because…" sentence. Pick the structure or theory that matches the dataset, state its function, and link it to the result.

Memory: levels of processing

Craik & Lockhart (1972): how deeply information is processed determines how well it is remembered.

LevelFocusExample taskMemory
Structural (shallowest)AppearanceIs the word in capital letters?Weakest
PhonologicalSoundDoes "lake" rhyme with "cake"?Medium
Semantic (deepest)MeaningDoes "apple" fit "she packed an __ for lunch"?Strongest

Craik & Tulving (1975) tested this: words processed for meaning (semantic) were recalled best.

Use it for: study methods, deep vs shallow, educative vs non-educative content. "Deep (semantic) processing creates stronger, more durable memory traces, so recall and test scores are higher."

Multi-store model (Atkinson & Shiffrin, 1968): sensory memory → (attention) → short-term memory (about 18–30 seconds, 7 ± 2 items) → (encoding / rehearsal) → long-term memory.

  • Maintenance rehearsal: repeating information to keep it in STM (shallow).
  • Elaborative rehearsal: linking new information to meaning and existing knowledge (deep), which transfers it to LTM.
  • Retrieval: bringing stored information back into conscious awareness.

Brain structures

Basal ganglia deep in the forebrain

Selects and initiates intended voluntary movements and inhibits competing (unwanted) movements. Also involved in procedural / habit memory.

Disruption → more competing movements, difficulty starting the intended movement.

Cerebellum hindbrain, back of the brain

Coordinates timing, balance, posture and fine motor movement. Stores implicit procedural memories (skills) and classically conditioned motor responses.

Use for: motor skill accuracy, coordination, reaction-based tasks.

Primary motor cortex frontal lobe

Initiates voluntary movement by sending messages via motor neurons to skeletal muscles. Each hemisphere controls the opposite side of the body.

Hippocampus medial temporal lobe

Encodes and consolidates new explicit (declarative) memories, transferring them to long-term memory. Also spatial memory.

Damage → can't form new explicit memories (anterograde amnesia), so poorer recall of new events.

Amygdala medial temporal lobe

Detects emotional and threatening stimuli and triggers the fear / stress response (more stress hormones). Strengthens consolidation of emotional memories.

"When the amygdala is more active, it triggers the stress response and increases the release of stress hormones, so perceived stress rises."

Prefrontal cortex front of the frontal lobe

Working memory, attention, planning and decision-making. Its function is reduced by sleep loss.

Use for sleep / attention data: "with less sleep, alertness and attention are reduced, so information is processed more slowly."

Language areas left hemisphere

Broca's area (frontal): speech production. Wernicke's area (temporal): language comprehension.

Practice strengthens neural pathways (neuroplasticity), so accuracy rises with more hours of practice.

Sensory cortices

Primary visual cortex (occipital lobe): vision. Primary auditory cortex (temporal lobe): hearing. Somatosensory cortex (parietal lobe): touch and body sensation.

Neurons nervous system

  • Sensory neurons carry information from receptors to the CNS.
  • Interneurons relay messages within the CNS.
  • Motor neurons carry messages from the CNS to muscles and glands.

Reaction time = sensory input → brain processing → motor output. Anything that slows processing (e.g. tiredness) slows reaction time.

These are reminders, not new content. If your teacher worded a function differently, use their wording in the exam.

Cheat sheet

Formulas

  • Mean = sum ÷ n
  • Median = middle value in order (even n: average the two middle)
  • Range = highest − lowest
  • IQR = Q3 − Q1 (middle 50%)
  • SE = SD ÷ √n
  • 95% CI = mean ± 1.96 × SE (≈ ± 2 SE)

Read the numbers

  • p < 0.05 → significant, unlikely due to chance
  • p ≥ 0.05 → not significant, could be chance
  • r sign = direction; |r| size = strength (≥ 0.7 strong)
  • Bigger SD / range / IQR = more dispersion = more variability and uncertainty
  • Same people, both conditions → paired t-test
  • Different people → unpaired t-test

Verbs in one line each

  • Calculate: working + answer + units
  • Identify: name it, quote the value, no explanation
  • Contrast: both values with "whereas", or the difference
  • Sequence: show each value, order least → greatest
  • Distinguish: direction and rate of each trend
  • Predict: first-to-last trendline, extend, give a value
  • Draw a conclusion: trend + data + psychology

3-mark conclusion template

[1] "As ___ increases, ___ increases/decreases (from ___ to ___)."

[1] "This is a strong positive/negative correlation (r = ___), and is significant (p = ___ < 0.05)."

[1] "This may be because [structure/theory] ___, so ___."

Traps

  • Forgot to order the data before the median
  • Compared r values by sign instead of size
  • Subgroup ranges all equal? Say "equal variability"
  • Wrote "proves". Use "may" and "suggests"
  • Answered about one condition when asked about both (or the reverse)
  • Several participants share the top value? Name them all
